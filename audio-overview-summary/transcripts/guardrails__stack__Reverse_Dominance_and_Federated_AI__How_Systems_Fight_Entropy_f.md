**Summary**

The audio overview discusses how both ancient human societies and modern artificial intelligence systems manage disorder (entropy) by enforcing mechanisms that suppress individual selfishness. In early egalitarian hunter‑gatherer groups, “reverse dominance” kept ambitious individuals in check through collective psychological pressure—mockery (“dung practice”) and social isolation rather than outright suppression of talent. When ridicule failed, the community resorted to execution as a political sanction, often delegated by close relatives to limit revenge cycles.

In contemporary AI, federated learning (federated averaging) mirrors this principle: devices perform local model updates using only their own data, sending aggregated gradients back without transmitting raw information. Differential privacy further protects individual privacy by adding calibrated noise, ensuring the central server cannot infer specific data points while still capturing overall patterns. This layered defense—local processing + temporary aggregation + differential privacy—mirrors the social sanctions of old: they collectively enforce a “reverse dominance” over potential power‑hungry elements.

The overview also introduces the Relativistic Staler Vector Plenum (RSVP) framework, which models order as emerging from three interacting fields—capacity (storage), vector flow (attention), and entropy (disorder)—and shows how cooperative intelligence in federated learning corresponds to higher stages of RSVP (pi‑4: distributed cooperation). The overarching theme is that any organized system must actively constrain disorder through mechanisms that suppress individual selfishness, whether via social sanctions or algorithmic safeguards.

**KEYWORDS:** reverse dominance hierarchy, ridicule, shunning, execution, federated averaging, differential privacy, collective intelligence, entropy management, RSVP framework, cooperative flux.
