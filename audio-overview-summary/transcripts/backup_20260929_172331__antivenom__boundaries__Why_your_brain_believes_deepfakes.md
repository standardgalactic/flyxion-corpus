# backup_20260929_172331/antivenom/boundaries/Why_your_brain_believes_deepfakes

**Summary**

The essay discusses how human cognition processes synthetic media (e.g., deepfakes) not through a truth‑checking mechanism but via a coherence‑driven memory system. Memory is reconstructive rather than reproductive, meaning we rebuild past experiences each time we recall them, using heuristic cues like perceptual richness and retrieval fluency to deem memories “real.” This leads the brain to accept synthetic content that feels highly coherent (low entropy) and near consistent with existing beliefs, bypassing any provenance checks. The paper introduces a computational model called CLIO architecture, which shows how confidence values are assigned without regard for source, allowing false information to be accepted as real.

Key vulnerabilities include:
- **Entropy:** Synthetic media’s high perceptual density reduces uncertainty (low entropy), making it easy for the brain to accept.
- **Obstruction:** Conflict with existing beliefs creates obstruction; however, synthetic content that is only slightly off can slip through because the mismatch isn’t severe enough to trigger rejection.
- **Near Consistency:** 65% similarity to known reality allows deepfakes to be accepted without raising alarms, leading to self‑reinforcing belief systems.

The essay argues that genuine defense against such manipulation requires social correction—interacting with people who hold different beliefs—to introduce friction and raise entropy/obstruction. Without varied input, exposure to identical synthetic content leads to correlated drift, accelerating reality deformation.
