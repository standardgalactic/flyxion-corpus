# antivenom/economics/Why_digital_platforms_mimic_war_economies

The key takeaway from this discussion is that the current digital platforms—whether they be gig work marketplaces, social media networks, or emerging artificial intelligence tools—are fundamentally designed around an extractive business model. This means they profit primarily from creating friction, uncertainty, and failure within user interactions (often referred to as "interface entropy"). The paper outlines a structural shift necessary for these systems to become non‑extractive:

1. **Identity as a Trajectory**: Instead of using a simple namespace (like usernames), identity should be represented by a trajectory—chronologically ordered verified events that demonstrate an individual’s history and capabilities. This makes it much harder for scammers or malicious actors to hijack identities, because the value lies in the actual outcomes rather than just the name.

2. **Distributed Interpretation**: By separating the substrate (a public, shared database of raw data) from multiple competing interpreters (algorithms that make sense of this data), platforms can avoid monopolizing relevance and visibility. Users could choose any interpreter to view their network or content without losing access to their underlying data.

3. **Refundable Bond System**: Replacing consumable tokens with refundable bonds ensures that platforms only profit from successful interactions where both parties benefit (e.g., a gig is completed). If a proposal fails, the bond is returned in full, preventing platforms from profiting off friction and uncertainty.

4. **Non‑Extractive Revenue Model**: Platforms must be designed so they cannot extract value from interface entropy—friction, failed matches, or user dissatisfaction. This aligns their survival with actual success rather than monetizing users’ struggles.

The paper concludes that while this alternative architecture is mathematically sound and could fundamentally change how platforms operate (especially in the context of AI), it faces a significant challenge: competing against extractive giants who can promise exponential returns to investors through high rent extraction. Building such systems requires not only technical innovation but also long-term strategic investment and community support, as they inherently run counter to current economic incentives.

In essence, the escape route from insidiousification involves redefining what success looks like for digital platforms—shifting focus from monetizing failure and uncertainty to fostering genuine value creation and user empowerment. This shift could potentially prevent AI tools (or any future technology) from becoming monopolistic choke points that control our cognitive capabilities. However, achieving this will require overcoming substantial barriers in both economics and market dynamics.
