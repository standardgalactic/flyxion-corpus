**How Laundering Works in Practice**

Laundering, in this context, refers to the process by which human agency—specifically the decisions and responsibilities of bureaucrats, engineers, and administrators—is obscured or transferred onto a technical artifact (like an algorithm) that appears neutral and unaccountable. Here’s how it plays out:

1. **Decision Making & Reframing**: When city officials adopt an algorithm for distributing housing vouchers, they are making a highly political decision about who gets help and who doesn’t. By framing this as a “technical optimization,” the messy social realities of resource allocation become abstracted into variables and objective functions.

2. **Human Choices Behind the Algorithm**:
   - **Bureaucrats**: Choose which data to use for training.
   - **Engineers**: Decide on the mathematical objective function (what success looks like).
   - **Administrators**: Set deployment thresholds (who qualifies).

3. **Transferring Responsibility**: If a family is denied housing, officials can say, “The algorithm denied you,” rather than admitting, “We decided you weren’t a priority.” This shifts political and moral responsibility onto the algorithm itself.

4. **Legitimacy & Accountability**: The algorithm becomes an unshakable authority figure—immune to public shaming or electoral accountability. It allows those in power to evade direct scrutiny for their decisions while maintaining the illusion of impartiality.

**Who Is Laundering What?**

- **From Human Bureaucrats to Algorithmic Artifacts**: They launder their agency by presenting algorithmic outcomes as neutral, objective results.
  
- **To Public Perception**: By attributing failures or denials to “the algorithm,” they obscure the human choices behind it and avoid direct accountability.

**Connecting to Historical Context (Fabius & Hannibal)**

The analogy with Fabius in ancient Rome illustrates how political rivals can manipulate narratives to delegitimize strategic decisions. Just as Varro’s aggressive tactics were framed as heroic action, modern systems are often portrayed as neutral tools rather than the products of human choices and biases.

**Call to Action**

- **Transparency & Accountability**: Demand radical transparency about training data, objective functions, and deployment thresholds.
  
- **Appeal Processes**: Ensure meaningful appeal processes for those affected by algorithmic decisions.

- **Legal Frameworks**: Hold institutional operators legally responsible, not the software itself.

By recognizing that any predictive system inherently contains bias (a built-in tendency), we must confront the reality of democratic governance in a technologically mediated world. The key is to ensure that societal outcomes are shaped deliberately and transparently by those who hold political power, rather than being silently absorbed into opaque algorithms.
