**Title: The Power of Excess Capacity in Learning and Intelligence**

**Introduction**

The concept of "excess capacity" challenges conventional wisdom about learning and intelligence. Traditionally, we are led to believe that simplifying our environment—removing unnecessary details—and focusing on core facts will enhance understanding. However, recent insights from neuroscience, biology, and artificial intelligence suggest the opposite: true brilliance may come from embracing complexity rather than discarding it.

**Biological Perspective**

1. **Brain Architecture**: The primary visual cortex (V1) in a cat illustrates this principle. For every one nerve fiber bringing visual data into the brain, there are 25 to 1 expansion ratios of projecting axons. This massive redundancy allows for rapid processing and robust generalization—critical when hunting or navigating complex environments.

2. **Metabolic Cost Argument**: Critics argue that building such a "gas-guzzling V8 engine" is inefficient biologically. However, the brain's metabolic cost is justified by its ability to quickly adapt and generalize, which are essential survival skills. The energy spent on this redundancy ensures fast decision-making under pressure.

**Artificial Intelligence Insights**

1. **CNNs vs. LLMs**: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) used in computer vision tasks operate within an excess capacity regime. They can memorize vast amounts of specific training images while maintaining generalization, much like the human visual system.

2. **Large Language Models (LLMs)**: Contrary to expectations, LLMs are operating in a state of constrained capacity relative to their enormous parameter count. Current scaling laws indicate they only perfectly memorize about 0.2%–1.5% of their training data, discarding the overwhelming majority of specific details.

3. **Hallucination Problem**: The inability of LLMs to recall exact facts (hallucinations) stems from this constraint. They compress vast amounts of information into generalized statistical rules rather than retaining detailed episodic memories, leading to inaccuracies when asked for precise historical or factual data.

**Implications for Human Learning**

1. **Decluttering vs. Embracing Complexity**: Modern society often promotes minimalism and the removal of noise from our environments—through curated news feeds, standardized education, and sleek designs. However, if Dubova and Sloman's framework is correct, we may be inadvertently starving ourselves of the complexity necessary for deep learning.

2. **The Value of Noise**: Our brains evolved to thrive in an excess capacity regime, processing massive amounts of noisy data efficiently. Removing this noise might hinder our ability to form robust generalizations and insights.

**Conclusion**

Embracing a little mess—allowing complex, seemingly irrelevant details into our daily lives—could be crucial for optimal learning and intelligence. By recognizing the value of excess capacity, we may unlock deeper understanding and creativity in both ourselves and artificial systems alike. This perspective encourages us to reconsider how we design environments, curate information, and approach education, potentially leading to more profound and lasting insights.
