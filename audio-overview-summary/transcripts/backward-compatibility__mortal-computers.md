# backward-compatibility/mortal-computers

The exploration of xenobots, fungal computing, organoid intelligence, and minimal models of mortal computation collectively underscores a profound shift toward integrating biological principles into computational paradigms. These examples illustrate how nature's efficient, resilient, and adaptive systems can inform the development of advanced artificial intelligence and robotics.

**Xenobots**: The self-healing properties and metabolic capabilities of xenobots demonstrate that it is possible to create machines capable of not only surviving but also thriving in their environments without external intervention. This aligns with the vision of mortal computation, where computational entities are designed to be autonomous, adaptive, and capable of sustained operation.

**Fungal Computing**: By leveraging the electrical activity within fungal mycelium networks, researchers can develop novel computational models that prioritize resilience, self-maintenance, and efficient information transmission. These biological systems offer a blueprint for creating energy-efficient computing architectures that mimic natural processes for problem-solving and adaptation.

**Organoid Intelligence**: The ability of organoids to engage in simple reinforcement learning tasks highlights the potential for merging biological structures with computational algorithms to achieve more adaptive and intelligent behavior. This could lead to breakthroughs in neuromorphic computing, where artificial systems are designed to learn and adapt over time, reflecting the principles of life-like intelligence.

**Minimal Models of Mortal Computation**: Concepts such as homeostatic predictive coding circuits (H-PCC) and metabolism-based chemotaxis provide a theoretical foundation for simulating life-like adaptability in computational models. These approaches emphasize the integration of internal regulatory processes with external environmental interactions, paving the way for systems that exhibit genuine autonomous behavior akin to biological organisms.

**Implications for Future Research**: The convergence of these diverse examples points toward an interdisciplinary research frontier where biology, chemistry, physics, and computer science collaborate to push the boundaries of what is possible in robotics and artificial intelligence. By studying how natural systems achieve resilience, self-organization, and adaptive learning, researchers can develop computational models that are not only responsive but also capable of evolving functionalities for survival and interaction with their environments.

**Conclusion**: The collective insights from xenobots, fungal computing, organoid intelligence, and minimal models of mortal computation collectively highlight a transformative potential in the field of artificial intelligence. By drawing inspiration from biological systems, researchers can design computational entities that are more adaptive, resilient, and capable of autonomous behavior—marking a significant step toward realizing truly life-like machines. This interdisciplinary approach not only enriches our understanding of computational paradigms but also opens new avenues for practical applications in robotics, AI, and beyond, where the boundaries between artificial systems and natural intelligence blur.
