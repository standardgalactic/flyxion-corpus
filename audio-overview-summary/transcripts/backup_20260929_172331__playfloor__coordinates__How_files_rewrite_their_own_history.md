**Template Capture**

Template capture occurs when an early dataset—often imperfect or biased—is used to create a template that becomes entrenched in the system. This flawed template works well enough for compression but remains embedded due to its computational efficiency, making it extremely difficult to replace even if better templates are discovered later. The system gets “stuck” in this local minimum because rewriting all affected files would be prohibitively expensive.

**Hallucinated Economy**

In lossy compression regimes (like JPEGs), the archive may become overly confident that a template is flawless and discard residual details, leading to hallucinations—rendering an image or data point that no longer matches reality. The distortion lattice forces the system to retain enough raw residuals so that any drift toward falsehood can be detected and corrected, acting as a safety net against such hallucinations.

**Scientific Resistance**

When new, superior templates (e.g., from LIDAR scans) are introduced but remain suppressed by entrenched older templates due to competition for computational resources, it mirrors human sociological resistance to paradigm shifts. This dynamic shows how deeply ingrained systems can resist change even when better data is available, reflecting the real-world phenomenon of academic or scientific resistance.

**Resonance Without a Substrate**

The final thought invites us to reconsider our memories and understanding: if memory functions like a rematching archive, then past experiences are not static recordings but continuously reinterpreted templates. This raises profound questions about identity—whether we truly remember raw events or merely render highly compressed, updated versions of them.

In essence, these concepts illustrate how compression algorithms can both preserve and distort reality through their design choices, emphasizing the need for mechanisms (like distortion lattices) that maintain a connection to empirical truth while optimizing storage efficiency.
