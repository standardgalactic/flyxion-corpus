# antivenom/monograph/Unfinishable_games_that_weaponize_your_sleep

**Title: The Architecture of Unfinishability – A Deep Dive into Digital Engagement**

---

### **Introduction**

In today’s digital landscape, many applications and games are designed to be "unfinishable"—meaning they encourage continuous engagement by making it difficult or costly for users to disengage. This concept is explored through the lens of a paper that examines how such systems exploit psychological and social structures to maintain user attachment and extraction.

### **Key Concepts**

1. **Unfinishability**: Refers to systems where players cannot safely disengage due to built-in mechanisms that keep them engaged indefinitely, often at a financial or time cost.
   
2. **Right to Absence**: A proposed design criterion that mandates any system must allow participants to invoke a bounded absence interval where their exposure drops below a tolerable threshold. This right is unilaterally granted—users should not need to rely on others’ goodwill.

3. **Temporal Extraction**: The practice of extracting value from users through time-based vulnerabilities, such as forcing players into vulnerable periods (e.g., night-time raids) that disrupt personal schedules and well-being.

### **Challenges in Implementation**

Implementing the "right to absence" without destroying the core experience of a game or persistent digital environment is extremely challenging. The paper walks through several flawed potential remedies:

- **Individual Shields**: Protect solo players but burden collective leaders, leading to inequitable distribution of responsibility.
  
- **Alliance Vulnerability Windows**: Setting specific attack windows (e.g., 6 PM–10 PM EST) fails globally due to differing time zones, causing sleep disruption for international members.

- **Asynchronous Attack Declarations**: Requiring a mandatory 24-hour warning before attacks reduces immediacy and thrill but may fundamentally lower the game’s stakes by turning it into a bureaucratic process.

- **Reversible Losses**: Making losses easily reversible (undoing base destruction with minimal fees) could solve extraction issues but would diminish the psychological impact of gameplay, potentially destroying its appeal.

### **Social vs. Regulatory Solutions**

Given the difficulty in designing software that balances persistence and safety without harming user experience, regulatory approaches are also considered:

- **Regulatory Measures**: Unlike loot boxes, which can be regulated like casino games due to their explicit monetary extraction, temporal extraction is invisible and harder to regulate.
  
- **Occupational Scheduling Protections**: Similar protections for gig economy workers or on-call staff could address the social cost of unfinishability in professional contexts.

### **Broader Implications**

The discussion extends beyond gaming:

- **Professional Environments**: Slack, Teams messages from bosses during non-working hours exploit similar psychological mechanisms.
  
- **Gig Economy and On-Call Staffing**: Continuous availability is demanded for financial stability, flattening natural circadian rhythms into a state of constant vigilance.

### **Conclusion**

The paper concludes that addressing unfinishability requires new vocabularies and regulatory frameworks to protect users from both monetary and temporal extraction. It emphasizes the need to defend future availability rights against systems that claim unlimited authority over user time based on group standing membership.

---

**Final Thoughts**

As we navigate increasingly digitalized daily lives, it’s crucial to question how these architectures affect our well-being and autonomy. The principles discussed—such as ensuring a "right to absence"—could serve as foundational guidelines for designing more sustainable and equitable digital experiences across various sectors beyond gaming.
