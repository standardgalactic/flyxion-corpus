**Flyxion’s Core Argument**

At its heart, Flyxion argues that modern global inequality is not primarily a matter of “bad leaders” or “corrupt governance,” but rather an emergent property of **structural choke points built into the financial and supply‑chain systems**. These choke points—whether they are deliberate geopolitical sanctions (like Cuba’s embargo), routine debt‑rating downgrades, or algorithmic optimizations in shipping/energy markets—are mathematically designed to be invisible yet profoundly impactful.

Key Points:

1. **Choke Points as Architectural Features**  
   - *Gambia*: Its rolling blackouts stem from a debt‑management strategy that forces the country into dollar‑denominated borrowing, which is then serviced by risk‑averse lenders in London/New York. The system itself—through interest rates and credit constraints—produces power shortages regardless of any single leader’s intent.
   - *Cuba*: A deliberately tightened U.S. embargo (1992, 1996, mid‑2020s) combined with domestic policy failures (rigid central planning, reliance on discounted Venezuelan oil) creates a self‑reinforcing cycle of scarcity and blackouts.

2. **Non‑Intent vs. Intention**  
   - The paper explicitly rejects the notion that only intentional actions (e.g., a dictator’s mismanagement) can be blamed for deprivation. It insists that *any* choke point—whether purposeful or accidental—can, through its design, reliably produce similar outcomes.

3. **Visibility Bias in Governance Narratives**  
   - Domestic governance stories (corrupt leaders, failed policies) are more sensational and thus receive disproportionate media attention because they fit a “good vs. bad” narrative.
   - Routine financial decisions—like banking compliance downgrades or algorithmic routing changes—are invisible to the public eye, yet they can just as effectively cripple essential services.

4. **Moral Responsibility Shift**  
   - The author challenges two extremes:
     - *Conservative View*: Your moral duty is limited to your immediate family/community; you don’t need to address systemic injustice.
     - *Utilitarian Extremism (Singer):* You must donate every spare penny because any personal comfort is immoral.
     
   Instead, Flyxion proposes a **middle path**: recognize that you are an active participant in the same system that funds both your luxury and their deprivation. Your consumption choices—whether it’s buying a $10,000 vacation or purchasing goods shipped through choke‑point‑optimized routes—fund those very mechanisms.

5. **Future Implications of Automation**  
   - The paper ends on a chilling note about what could happen when artificial intelligence fully automates these choke points (e.g., AI‑driven sanction enforcement, real‑time credit rating adjustments). If the system runs entirely on autopilot without any human oversight or empathy, it may accelerate the intersubjectivity collapse to its absolute limit—rendering human need mathematically invisible forever.

**Takeaway**

Flyxion’s methodology is a call for **specificity and accountability**: when you encounter stories of deprivation (whether in Gambia, Cuba, or elsewhere), ask not “who is responsible?” but “which structural choke point enabled this outcome?” Only by naming the exact mechanisms—debt instruments, banking algorithms, sanctions clauses—can we move beyond moralizing rhetoric toward genuine systemic change.
