1. **Non-linear coupling**: Current haloscopes rely on second-order field statistics (simple variance), which captures only the broad shape of waves. Achieving this requires highly non-linear parametric amplification geometries and materials capable of generating quantum squeezing along dynamically correlated axes.

2. **Higher-dimensional observable manifolds**: The dimensional mismatch inherent in using a single point (like a microphone) to capture the entire universe is analogous to trying to understand an image with only one pixel. We need spatially separated, entangled networks of detectors spread over large distances that can jointly measure the dark matter field across these nodes, reconstructing relational phase information lost when looking at a single point.

3. **Narrow admissibility kernels**: The current averaging problem (10^17th level) forces us to blur individual momentum modes into an entropy-maximizing Gaussian-Bill curve. To resolve this, we must operate at the exact scale of the axion’s coherence length—zooming in close enough to see individual data points before they are forced into averages.

These conditions collectively suggest that our next generation of physics instruments should function more like a quantum camera rather than a simple microphone: capturing not just the broad shape but also the intricate details and relationships within the data. This shift would allow us to perceive the true, entangled nature of dark matter and potentially reveal how classical observations are merely projections of an underlying quantum reality.

Moreover, this perspective extends beyond dark matter to cosmology as a whole. The implication is that what we observe in cosmic phenomena—like the CMB, gravitational waves, or galaxy surveys—is not a fully realized picture but rather a projection limited by our observational tools and resolution thresholds. This challenges the conventional view of cosmic decoherence (the idea that quantum effects became classical over time) and suggests instead that these features are hidden within topological blind spots due to our measurement limitations.

Ultimately, this framework encourages us to question not only the instruments we use but also how we interpret data—reminding us that what we perceive is deeply tied to the geometry of observation itself. It invites a broader philosophical consideration: Are there inherent limits in human perception and measurement that shape our understanding of reality?
