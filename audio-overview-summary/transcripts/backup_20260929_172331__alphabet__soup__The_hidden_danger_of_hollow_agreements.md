**Argument Summary**

The argument distinguishes between two types of logical agreements: *coherent* (or “green light”) and *hollow agreement*. In coherent cases, multiple pieces of evidence independently confirm a proposition without contradiction. This is analogous to cameras on either side of a bridge both indicating it’s open—each provides independent verification that aligns with the physical reality.

In contrast, hollow agreement occurs when two systems or pieces of evidence agree on a truth value but do so for structurally incompatible reasons. Using the digital credential analogy: if one subsystem validates an ID using cryptographic key X and another uses key Y (which should not be equivalent), the mere fact that both say “valid” is misleading because the underlying coherence is broken.

**Why It Matters**

Classical logic, which treats agreement as sufficient proof of truth (i.e., “one equals one”), fails to account for *evidence coherence*. Acting on hollow agreements—where the reasons behind agreement are fundamentally different—can lead to disastrous consequences. For instance, allowing a hacker into a secure system by trusting two contradictory validation methods is catastrophic.

**Flyxion’s Repair Cost Formula**

To address this issue, Flyxion introduces a repair cost formula:

\[ J_A = E_A + \lambda R_A + \mu C_A \]

- **\(E_A\)**: Baseline effort required to take the action.
- **\(\lambda R_A\)**: Penalty for acting on contradictions (glut).
- **\(\mu C_A\)**: Action relative coherence liability, measuring how structurally fragmented evidence is.

The coefficient \(\mu\) quantifies how sensitive a specific action is to this fragmentation. For actions like bridge crossing, the penalty is negligible because physical reality aligns with logical agreement. However, for authentication (high-stakes security), even minor inconsistencies can be disastrous, making \(\mu\) large and penalties high.

**Limitations & Open Problems**

Flyxion’s framework successfully models many cases but fails to map contradictions onto a geometric concept called *non-zero-each cohomology class*, which is analogous to the impossibility of gluing local patches (like an infinite staircase) into a coherent global structure. This failure underscores that contradiction and coherence are independent, not equivalent.

The manuscript leaves open the problem of how to transition between different logical regimes (e.g., from classical logic to paraconsistent logic). Understanding when and how such regime changes occur is crucial for navigating reality’s borders where rules may shift dramatically—much like understanding the laws during the Planck second after the Big Bang.

**Practical Implication**

In everyday life, we often encounter hollow agreements: people agreeing on outcomes without shared underlying reasons. Recognizing this can prevent costly errors in decision-making, from politics to personal relationships, by prompting us to verify coherence rather than relying solely on agreement.
