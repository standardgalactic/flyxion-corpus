**Summary of Key Points**

1. **Capitalism as a Dynamical System:**  
   - The global economy is modeled as a complex dynamical system with attractors representing stable states (e.g., the “valley” of capitalist stability).  
   - When faced with crises—financial, legitimation, or ecological—the system can either adjust within its current attractor or undergo a bifurcation, leading to a new attractor.

2. **First Two Crises and Their Solutions:**  
   - **Financial Crisis (e.g., 2008):** Managed by scaling up the model; core countries extract surplus from peripheral developing nations through offshoring and labor arbitrage.  
   - **Legitimation Crisis:** Occurs when political consent erodes, viewing the state as merely an enforcer of inequality rather than a provider of public goods. The system responds by exporting exploitation to less regulated regions.

3. **Third Crisis: Ecological Crisis**  
   - Proven irreversible by thermodynamics (second law), which states that nature is not an infinite free resource but a materially closed system with entropy constraints.  
   - This crisis cannot be “printed away” like monetary solutions; it leads to irreversible collapse if background conditions fall below critical biological thresholds.

4. **Transitioning Out of the Attractor:**  
   - Simply changing personnel (e.g., better central bankers) does not alter structural imperatives because the underlying equations remain unchanged.  
   - Social movements act as bifurcation-seeking perturbations, forcing a qualitative change in system behavior by dissolving hidden subsidies and revealing true costs.

5. **Four Alternative Geometries for Post‑Capitalist Society:**  
   1. **Cooperative Ownership:** Replaces the accumulation attractor with democratic allocation of surplus, shifting optimization from profit to social utility.  
   2. **Public Provisioning & Commons:** Breaks the inequality constraint by guaranteeing basic human needs (healthcare, housing, education, UBI) outside market mechanisms, effectively handing everyone a raft instead of an ocean liner.  
   3. **Participatory Planning:** Undoes semantic entropy reduction by expanding policy dimensions, allowing transparent democratic institutions to plan macroeconomics rather than relying on hidden controllers.  
   4. **Ecological Embedding:** Imposes strict steady‑state constraints ensuring resource use and waste emissions never exceed natural renewal rates, closing the open boundary and respecting thermodynamic laws.

6. **Constructive Dimension of Transformation:**  
   - Transitioning requires building an alternative attractor (new cooperative channels, robust public commons, democratic provisioning methods) before breaking the current system.  
   - Without this groundwork, crossing the bifurcation point leads to systemic collapse rather than a utopian outcome; it results in unmitigated chaos and total systemic failure.

**Final Thought:**  
Viewing economic phenomena through the lens of underlying mathematical equations—rather than superficial narratives—highlights how hidden dependencies (unpaid care work, community resources, ecological health) are integral to maintaining current systems. Recognizing these dynamics encourages proactive steps toward constructing new frameworks that respect both human and environmental limits, ensuring a more resilient future beyond mere survival in an unsustainable equilibrium.
