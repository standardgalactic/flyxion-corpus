# laboratory/projects/How_Minds_Rewrite_the_Menu_of_Possibilities

**Summary**

The discussion centers on a philosophical and mathematical framework that distinguishes between two kinds of errors in systems—whether they are human or artificial:

1. **Ordinary Errors (Wrong Position):** These occur when a system is simply misaligned within its existing space of possibilities. The solution typically involves adjusting the precision weighting, i.e., refining guesses or evidence without fundamentally changing the underlying structure.

2. **Fundamental Errors (Wrong Space):** This happens when the entire space of possibilities itself needs to be redefined—akin to rewriting the rules of a game entirely. Recognizing this requires a meta‑level revision that goes beyond mere statistical adjustments.

The Chinese Restaurant Process (CRP) is used as an illustrative model for understanding how systems can grow their “latent categories” (or tables) without necessarily undergoing true domain revision, which Flyxion argues the CRP does not achieve because its growth rules are pre‑programmed. This distinction helps clarify why Bayesian models might be seen as merely sophisticated versions of precision weighting rather than genuine instances of operator A.

A second objection—**the Infinite Regress Objection**—questions whether any such meta‑revision could ever truly settle the issue, suggesting a never-ending chain of higher operators (γ mapping to γ′). Flyxion counters this by emphasizing that having multiple levels of rules does not inherently imply an infinite regress; each level can operate independently without needing a grand unifying theory. This is analogous to how first-order logic functions without requiring second‑order quantification.

Finally, the conversation touches on practical applications: recognizing whether one’s persistent problem (e.g., in work or relationships) requires shifting from merely adjusting evidence (precision weighting) to fundamentally redefining what counts as a valid solution (operator A). This self‑diagnostic approach encourages listeners to pause and consider if they are dealing with an ordinary error versus a fundamental boundary issue.

The overarching takeaway is that the distinction between “wrong position” and “wrong space” offers a powerful diagnostic tool for personal, professional, and even biological decision-making processes. It challenges us to question not just what we see but also which possibilities our cognitive architectures actively refuse to entertain.
