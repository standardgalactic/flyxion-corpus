# portable-warrant/Why_one_success_proves_absolutely_nothing

The core takeaway is that we must shift our approach from relying solely on snapshots—i.e., confirming a system’s performance in one moment—to conducting continuation tests that verify whether a system can maintain its desired state over time under varying conditions and perturbations. This involves:

1. **Demanding the Tuple**: Whenever capacity claims are made (whether for technology, policy, or personal abilities), ask for the full tuple—specific variations of input, temporal horizons, types of perturbations tested, and hidden/internal states that affect tolerance.

2. **Understanding True Capacity vs. Snapshot Success**: Recognize that a system appearing to succeed in one snapshot does not guarantee it can endure over time; true capacity is about maintaining performance under stress and changing conditions, which requires structural reserve (the “distance to the boundary”).

3. **Applying Continuation Tests**: Use continuation tests rather than mere existence checks. These tests probe how well a system persists when pushed beyond comfortable parameters—essentially verifying if it can adapt to unforeseen changes or disturbances.

4. **Acknowledging Limits in Historical Systems**: For historical sciences like geology, where retrospective verification is possible (e.g., asteroid impacts), we must accept that continuation testing for engineered systems requires prospective stress-testing and active probing of system boundaries.

5. **Embracing the Cost of True Capacity**: Understand that true capacity involves necessary costs—time, space, energy—that enable adaptation and resilience. These are not inefficiencies but essential features of genuine capability.

By applying these principles, we move from superficial claims of success to a deeper understanding of what it truly means for something—or someone—to have lasting, adaptable capacity in the face of uncertainty and change.
