**English Summary**

The discussion centers on how Flyxion—a theoretical model—explains the cognitive processes behind language stability. It contrasts two types of morphological changes:

1. **Depth Zero Changes**: These are straightforward, like adding a dot to a letter (e.g., “run” → “ran”). They can be undone easily by simply removing or correcting the change without altering deeper structures.

2. **Depth One Changes**: These involve fundamental shifts in generative rules, such as moving from context-sensitive ligatures to a fixed four-position system for letters. Undoing these changes is fundamentally different because it requires restoring lost generative machinery and cognitive patterns that were previously established.

**Key Points:**

- **Rote Memorization vs. Generative Rules**: The past tense “ran” is learned through memorization rather than applying a mathematical rule, illustrating how some linguistic rules are stabilized by lexical memory rather than computation.
  
- **Interrogative Lookup Model**: Flyxion frames retrieving broken plurals as an “interrogative lookup” against stored tables in the brain, emphasizing that not all morphological stabilization is purely computational.

- **Cost-Benefit Framework**: The traditional flat cost-benefit model (S = αD – βC) assumes reversibility of changes symmetrically. However, Flyxion argues this fails for depth one changes because reversing them involves restoring complex generative rules and lost cognitive structures.

**Implications:**

- **Reversibility Matters**: Undoing a depth zero change is simple; undoing a depth one change requires rebuilding the entire system’s generative framework.
  
- **Structural Layers in Complex Systems**: Flyxion highlights that modifying surface features (depth zero) differs from altering deep generative rules (depth one), which has profound implications for understanding and managing complex systems like language, business processes, or AI models.

**Final Thought:**

The exploration of depth in cognitive architecture reveals a critical question about the future reliance on opaque artificial intelligence models. If their underlying generative mechanisms remain hidden, we risk losing the ability to assess what changes are reversible or costly, potentially leading to irreversible shifts in communication and thought patterns. This underscores the importance of transparency and teachability in AI systems designed to interact with human language.
