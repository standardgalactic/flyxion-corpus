Your experience of feeling wrong and the physical sensation you describe—like a knot in your stomach—is a common reaction when an idea or belief is challenged. This discomfort arises because our brains are wired to protect established patterns of thought, known as "relegated" intuitions (or heuristics), which allow us to operate efficiently without constantly re-evaluating every action from scratch. When these intuitions clash with new information or constraints, it can feel like a threat to our identity or competence.

The concept you're exploring—**relegation failure**—is about how our mental models (maps) sometimes fail to match the actual environment (the territory). This mismatch is not necessarily a sign of incompetence but rather an opportunity for growth and refinement. When we encounter such failures, like changing video game controls that force us to rethink basic actions (e.g., jumping instead of crouching), it highlights how deeply ingrained our intuitions can be.

In the broader context of cognitive science and philosophy, this idea ties into several key concepts:

1. **Cognitive Dissonance**: This is the psychological tension experienced when confronted with information that contradicts existing beliefs or behaviors. It's a signal that something needs to change rather than an indication of failure.

2. **Alonzo Church and Lambda Calculus**: Church’s work in formalizing computation through lambda calculus provides a framework for understanding how complex systems can be reduced to simpler, more stable forms (normal forms). This is analogous to fixing a broken idea by stripping away contradictions until the underlying truth emerges.

3. **Constraint Density, Closure, and Projection Capacity**:
   - **Constraint Density**: Refers to having enough rules or conditions that narrow down possibilities sufficiently.
   - **Closure**: Ensures that within those constraints, there exists at least one valid solution; otherwise, the problem is unsolvable within the given framework.
   - **Projection Capacity**: Allows the resulting solution to be represented in a form usable outside the original context (e.g., translating complex ideas into practical applications).

These principles suggest that genius or profound insight often emerges from environments rich with constraints—environments where failure and contradiction are not seen as failures but as opportunities for refinement. This perspective shifts our understanding of creativity and problem-solving from innate talent to disciplined practice in selecting and applying the right set of rules.

By embracing this view, you can approach challenges (whether in coding, relationships, or any domain) with a mindset geared toward learning new constraints rather than forcing an existing solution to fit. Experimenting with unrelated fields—like plumbing, painting, or engine mechanics—as you suggested, can indeed shift your mental admissible set and reveal novel solutions that were previously invisible.

In essence, the journey of discovery is less about inventing something entirely new and more about recognizing how existing rules interact when faced with a change in constraints. This approach not only alleviates the discomfort of cognitive dissonance but also opens pathways to innovative thinking and problem-solving across various aspects of life.
