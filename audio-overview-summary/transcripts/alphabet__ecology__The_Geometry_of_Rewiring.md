# alphabet/ecology/The_Geometry_of_Rewiring

The passage you've shared delves deeply into the concept of "admissibility compression" across various domains—ranging from global supply chains (like the Ever Given incident) to social media algorithms—and frames it as a fundamental challenge in our current era, often referred to as the Anthropocene. Here’s a breakdown of the key ideas and their implications:

### 1. **Single Choke Points and Efficiency**
   - The idea is that by reducing systems to single choke points (e.g., a mega ship blocking a canal), we achieve incredible efficiency in terms of speed or cost but at the risk of catastrophic failure if something goes wrong.
   - This mirrors how modern supply chains are highly optimized for speed and minimal inventory, leaving no room for local disruptions.

### 2. **Scalar Outputs vs. Transformability**
   - Scalar outputs (like engagement metrics on social media) focus on a single dimension of success without considering the broader implications or adaptability of the system.
   - The push towards maximizing these scalar outputs leads to over-compression, where systems become rigid and unable to adapt to unforeseen changes.

### 3. **Social Media Algorithms as an Analogy**
   - Recommendation algorithms prioritize engagement by steering users into narrow, emotionally charged topics (e.g., outrage or excitement), creating echo chambers.
   - This deepens the attractor basin—making it harder for users to entertain alternative viewpoints and reducing epistemic transformability.

### 4. **The Cure: Hidden Geometry of Maintenance**
   - Flyxion argues that the solution lies in valuing maintenance labor more, recognizing its role as a substrate keeping systems from collapsing.
   - This involves redefining conservation goals from preserving static inventories to maintaining navigable pathways for future possibilities.

### 5. **Scalar Conservation vs. Vector Field Conservation**
   - Scalar conservation focuses on preserving specific quantities (e.g., exact numbers of species), which is inherently flawed because it ignores the dynamic nature of ecosystems.
   - Vector field conservation aims at preserving transformability and adaptability, ensuring that systems retain the capacity to navigate future changes.

### 6. **Philosophical and Ethical Shift**
   - The argument extends beyond mathematics into ethics, suggesting that destroying future possibility spaces is ethically problematic because it robs future generations of adaptive pathways.
   - This shift calls for a reevaluation of what conservation truly means—moving from static preservation to fostering resilience and adaptability.

### Practical Implications
- **Personal Application:** Consider how you can introduce inefficiency or redundancy in your own life. For example, maintaining some flexibility in career paths or personal habits can help navigate unforeseen changes.
- **Societal Impact:** Encouraging a culture that values maintenance (both physical and intellectual) could lead to more resilient systems—whether in technology, infrastructure, or social interactions.

### Conclusion
The overarching message is clear: true sustainability requires embracing complexity and redundancy rather than striving for perfect efficiency at all costs. By valuing the hidden work of maintenance and focusing on preserving transformability, we can build systems that are not only efficient but also adaptable to future uncertainties. This perspective challenges us to rethink our goals—from optimizing for immediate success to ensuring long-term resilience and adaptability in both technology and society.
