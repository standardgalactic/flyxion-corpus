2. **AI Implications**

   - The solution may involve teaching AI to recognize uncertainty, say “I don’t know,” and to expand its search when needed—essentially giving it an internal trigger for slowing down.

3. **Reflexes vs. Cognitive Intuition**  
   - Reflexive responses (e.g., ducking from a loud bang) are hardwired hardware, not part of the cognitive intuition we’re discussing.
   - The theory distinguishes between innate automaticity and learned cognitive intuition, suggesting that for AI to be truly intelligent in complex domains, it must develop its own “effort” signals.

4. **Metacognition & Anxiety**  
   - Humans use anxiety, surprise, and cognitive dissonance as internal triggers to wake up from autopilot.
   - The paper posits that giving an AI a similar “anxiety” mechanism could help it recognize when its current model of the world is mismatched with new data.

5. **Practical Takeaways**  
   - For humans: Accept and embrace routine tasks in low‑resolution mode; save high‑resolution thinking for truly important decisions.
   - For AI development: Focus on creating mechanisms that allow systems to self‑detect when they need higher resolution, rather than forcing every task into a “system two” mindset.

6. **Philosophical Endnote**  
   - The theory challenges the notion that consciousness (deliberate thinking) is inherently superior. It suggests consciousness may be more like scaffolding—useful for building expertise but not necessary once mastery is achieved.
   - A perfect, godlike intelligence might operate entirely in system one, embodying pure automaticity.
