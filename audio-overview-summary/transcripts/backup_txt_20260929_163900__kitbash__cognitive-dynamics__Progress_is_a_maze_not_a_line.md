**Synthesis Summary**

The core theme of Fliction’s general theory of admissibility is that capability—whether in technology, science, or society—is not merely a function of what is theoretically possible but rather a directed, indexed transition geometry shaped by the maze of constraints we navigate. Progress hinges on which paths are physically, energetically, and institutionally open from our current position, influenced by historical choices, resource budgets, and institutional gatekeepers.

Key points include:

1. **Constraints as Productive Mechanisms**  
   - Constraints (e.g., FDA safety gates, peer review) focus effort, preventing wasteful exploration of unproductive or dangerous avenues. They make viable solutions tractable rather than allowing endless experimentation in the vast space of possibilities.

2. **Tacit Knowledge vs. Legibility**  
   - Legibility—having explicit instructions—is neither necessary nor sufficient for reconstructability. Roman concrete’s recipe was legible yet failed to be reproduced due to missing tacit knowledge (the chemistry behind seawater reactions). Conversely, experimental archaeology shows that capability can be fully reconstructed without any written texts by relying on physical practice and apprenticeship.

3. **Preservation of Capability**  
   - Societies must maintain both explicit documentation and living practices. Relying solely on digital archives or manuals risks losing the ability to rebuild critical systems (e.g., Byzantine Greek fire) when engineers retire without training replacements.

4. **Implications for AI and Future Breakthroughs**  
   - The rise of artificial intelligence raises a cautionary thought: if we accept perfect legibility from AI—complete blueprints for breakthrough technologies—we risk losing the tacit knowledge needed to implement, maintain, or repair those solutions. This mirrors the Roman concrete builders’ situation where they possessed instructions but lacked the underlying chemistry.

**Final Thought**

The lesson is to view constraints not as limitations but as essential geometry that shapes productive progress. Embracing strict limitations can concentrate focus and lead to breakthroughs, just as constrained environments (like CNNs or regulatory bodies) have historically facilitated innovation. The future of civilization may hinge on balancing the availability of perfect blueprints with the preservation of tacit knowledge and practical skills necessary for implementation and resilience.
