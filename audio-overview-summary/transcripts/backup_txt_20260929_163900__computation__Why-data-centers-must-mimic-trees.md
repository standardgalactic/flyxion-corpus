The paper establishes that when the diffusion to reaction ratio (Da) falls below approximately 36%, the entire terrestrial compute infrastructure becomes unstable, regardless of how well you manage thermal reintegration (T) or degradation to repair ratios (Dr). This threshold is derived from their PDE model which simulates continuous interactions between hardware (rho), heat (theta), and wear (M) over time. 

In practical terms, if the rate at which spare parts, technicians, and capital can diffuse across geographic distances falls below 36%, it means that even with optimal thermal management and repair processes in place, the system will still experience a net loss of functionality due to inefficiencies in resource distribution. This is because degradation (M) outpaces the ability to react to it (Dr), leading to an overall expansion of entropy within the system—something we want to avoid for stability.

The 36% line serves as a critical benchmark: any infrastructure that cannot maintain Da above this threshold will eventually collapse under its own wear and tear, regardless of how efficiently heat is managed or how quickly repairs can be made. This underscores the importance of not only focusing on individual components like hardware efficiency (T) but also ensuring robust logistical networks to support rapid diffusion across regions.

In essence, while optimizing each dial individually—thermal reintegration, degradation repair ratio, and diffusion reaction ratio—is crucial, maintaining Da above 36% becomes a non-negotiable condition for sustaining stable compute systems on Earth.
