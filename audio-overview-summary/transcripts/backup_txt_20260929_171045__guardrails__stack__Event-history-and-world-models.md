The core message here revolves around a fundamental tension between economic incentives and cognitive preservation in digital storage systems. The argument highlights how current cloud storage platforms—like Gmail, Google Drive, and similar services—charge users based on apparent volume (the amount of space a file appears to take up) rather than informational entropy (how much unique non-redundant data it actually contains). This pricing model systematically penalizes the preservation of long, iterative, and branching histories, which are often cognitively valuable but contain a lot of redundant information. As a result, users are financially compelled to prune these histories prematurely, leading to what is termed "structural memory loss."

Key points include:

1. **Volume vs. Entropy**: Storage pricing that scales with apparent volume treats storage as expensive regardless of the actual unique content it holds, whereas informational entropy measures how much new, non-redundant information a file contains. This mismatch incentivizes users to delete or compress their data aggressively.

2. **Historical Example - Gmail**: The original promise of Gmail's gigabyte storage in 2004 encouraged users to retain extensive email histories due to the perceived abundance and low cost of storage. However, Google later introduced artificial scarcity by imposing a rigid shared quota without changing the underlying pricing model, leading many to delete old emails and drafts.

3. **Content Addressable Storage (CAS) & Deduplication**: Major cloud providers use CAS and deduplication internally, meaning identical files are stored once regardless of how many users upload them. Yet, they charge for each copy as if it were unique, creating a rent-extraction mechanism based on the illusion of scarcity.

4. **Information Feudalism**: This economic model treats memory as expensive, forcing premature collapse of event histories and leading to loss of long-term cognitive structures. The proposed counter-architecture, Plenum Hub, would charge based on informational novelty rather than volume, aligning incentives with preserving history.

5. **Plenum Hub**: A cooperative counter-architecture that uses existing mature technologies like CAS and delta compression but charges users based on the actual new information generated (entropy). This model reduces financial penalties for iterative branching reasoning and treats shared knowledge as a collective asset, making it cheaper to store everything long-term.

6. **Architectural Commutativity**: Systems optimized for control (like current cloud services) force early context collapse, leading to brittle single-threaded hierarchies that are optimized only for control. In contrast, systems like Git or complex interactive programming environments preserve history as an authoritative asset, allowing for coherent and lawful composition of solutions over time.

7. **Implications**: The choice between these architectures affects not just how we store data but also our cognitive agency—whether we can think complex, coherent thoughts without being compelled to delete parts of our historical thought processes due to arbitrary quotas or economic pressures.

In essence, the argument calls for a shift from current economic models that tax memory and force early deletion toward systems that value history as an essential non-negotiable asset, preserving structural integrity necessary for robust cognition.
