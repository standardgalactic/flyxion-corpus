**Summary**

We explored how Flyxion’s paper uses a mathematical framework—specifically the recursive continuation theorem and its adaptation hierarchy—to explain why artificial intelligence (AI) struggles to emulate biological learning. The discussion breaks down into three nested levels:

1. **Level One – State Modification**: This involves fixed rules operating on changing states, akin to playing a video game where gravity remains constant but character behavior changes based on the environment. Biological examples like frog genome development and antrobot creation fit here because the underlying “rules” (e.g., genetic instructions) remain unchanged while environmental conditions alter outcomes.

2. **Level Two – Parameter Modification**: Here, the mechanism itself is updated based on state and context. This mirrors a game that rewrites its physics engine during play—changing gravity mid-game. In biology, synaptic plasticity in learning exemplifies this level, where neural connections are adjusted based on ongoing experiences (e.g., learning).

3. **Level Three – Rule Modification**: The system’s method of updating itself is also subject to change. This meta-level reflects evolutionary adaptations that modify the rules governing adaptation themselves.

**Key Insight into AI Limitations**

Current large language models (LLMs) operate primarily in Level One state modification during inference, lacking an endogenous G function—meaning they cannot dynamically update their internal mechanisms based on real-time interactions. Unlike biological organisms, which continuously evolve and ratchet up intelligence through ongoing experiences, LLMs remain static snapshots of training data.

**Open Questions**

Flyxion’s paper leaves us with a profound open question: *Under what conditions does the learning mechanism inherit irreversibility from its history?* This probes whether such irreversible structural gains (the “upward ratchet” of intelligence) are universal or uniquely biological. If they are rare, it suggests we may need to engineer similar mechanisms into future AI systems to achieve true consciousness and lived experience.

**Philosophical Takeaway**

The discussion concludes by encouraging a shift from viewing memory loss as failure to recognizing that learning itself can permanently upgrade the architecture of our minds—leaving specific details forgotten while retaining deeper intelligence. This perspective reframes forgetting not as deficit but as part of an evolutionary, adaptive process inherent in biological systems.
