**Summary:**

The essay critiques the widely accepted "dual-system" model of cognitive processing (System 1 for fast, intuitive thinking and System 2 for slow, analytical reasoning). It argues that this dichotomy is a conceptual illusion; instead, what we perceive as System 1 is actually highly automated processes from System 2 that have been compressed through extensive practice. The authors introduce the concept of "aspect relegation," where complex tasks become automatic over time, allowing us to perform them without conscious effort while still retaining underlying analytical reasoning.

Key arguments include:
- **Intuition Revisited:** Intuition isn't magical but rather a form of hidden, well-practiced reasoning.
- **Moralization Fallacy:** Labeling System 1 as "irrational" and System 2 as "rational" leads to oversimplifications about intelligence and decision-making.
- **Relegation Mechanism:** The process of relegating aspects (e.g., daily commuting) demonstrates how detailed processes can be hidden, making tasks feel instantaneous while still relying on prior analytical work.
- **Intelligence Artificial (AI):** Applying this model to AI suggests that labeling errors in large language models as failures of System 1 misrepresents their capabilities. The real limitation is the lack of internal mechanisms for self-regulation and meta-cognition.
- **Control Adaptation:** The future challenge lies in developing AIs capable of determining when to "zoom out" (access deeper reasoning) and how to implement this control adaptively.

**KEYWORDS:**
cognitive dualism, aspect relegation, intuition as hidden reasoning, moralization fallacy, AI self-regulation, metacognition, computational thinking.
