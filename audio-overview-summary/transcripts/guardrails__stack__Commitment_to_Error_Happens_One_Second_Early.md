# guardrails/stack/Commitment_to_Error_Happens_One_Second_Early

The insights presented here fundamentally reshape our understanding of decision-making and cognitive control. Traditionally, we view errors as accidental slips that occur after a commitment has been made—akin to hitting the brakes on a runaway train. However, this new framework reveals that these “errors” are actually well‑executed plans whose outcomes happen to be penalized by external circumstances.

Key takeaways include:

1. **Early Commitment**: Decisions and subsequent errors (ICNs) occur within the first 200 milliseconds—a window where the brain has already committed to a trajectory, not merely reacting to evidence drift as classical models like the diffusion model DDM suggest.

2. **Proactive Suppression of Trajectories**: Real cognitive control isn’t about catching mistakes at the last second but about preemptively suppressing disfavored future trajectories. This proactive suppression is akin to pruning branches in a tree of possibilities, eliminating paths that lead to undesirable outcomes before they can fully develop.

3. **Reframed Agency**: Traditional agency—viewed as a momentary choice—is replaced by an understanding that agency involves shaping the space of possible futures over time through learning and practice. It’s no longer about making isolated choices but about continuously reconfiguring one’s neural architecture to favor beneficial trajectories.

4. **Redefinition of Rationality**: Rationality shifts from being defined as maximizing utility or computing the best answer to being measured by the avoidance of numerous suboptimal paths. Success becomes synonymous with effectively suppressing a vast array of potential failures rather than merely achieving optimal outcomes in any given moment.

5. **Connection to Active Inference**: The framework dovetails with Kari Friston’s Active Inference, which posits that the brain minimizes surprise by inferring future states. However, while Active Inference focuses on hypothetical policies, the admissible history framework provides the structural foundation—defining which futures are even possible to consider.

6. **Implications for Control**: The most profound form of self‑control isn’t about last-second interventions but involves cultivating neural architecture that physically prunes undesirable future trajectories. This is a quiet, background process of adjusting biases, learning rates, and overall brain dynamics to ensure the set of possible futures aligns with long-term goals.

In essence, this perspective suggests that true control lies in shaping the landscape of possibilities before any decision appears, rather than reacting to errors after they’ve been made. It underscores the importance of proactive, structural changes in our neural pathways as a means of achieving lasting self‑control and rationality.
