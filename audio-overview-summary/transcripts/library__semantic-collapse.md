# library/semantic-collapse

**Summary**

The audio overview discusses Stephen Henry McRae’s essay “How the Presumption of Atheism by Way of Semiotic Square of Opposition Leads to a Semantic Collapse.” The central thesis is that redefining atheism as merely the absence (or non‑belief) in God—rather than asserting its non‑existence—creates semantic confusion. Using Flew’s distinction between strong and weak atheism, McRae argues that if atheism includes both “not believing” and “disbelieving,” then a person could simultaneously be classified as an atheist, theist, and agnostic. This overlap—what he calls a *semantic collapse*—blurs the distinct meanings of key terms (atheism, theism, agnosticism) within philosophical discourse.

McRae employs a **semiotic square of opposition** to illustrate how logical relationships between opposing concepts become ambiguous under this redefinition. The essay highlights that such ambiguity undermines clear communication in debates about belief systems and suggests that preserving precise terminology is essential for productive dialogue.

Key arguments include:
- Flew’s view on atheism as “lack of belief” versus a positive claim of non‑existence.
- Use of the semiotic square to show how definitions interact via truth functions, leading to contradictory classifications (e.g., being both atheist and theist).
- Implications for everyday conversation: misinterpretations arise when terms lack clear boundaries.

The essay concludes with McRae advocating for semantic clarity to maintain meaningful distinctions in philosophical debate. Unresolved questions revolve around whether alternative definitions of atheism could be more philosophically sound without sacrificing precision, and how such shifts might affect broader discussions on belief systems.
