The key takeaway from these two papers is that our current systems—whether in computing, economics, or everyday life—are fundamentally designed to optimize for speed and efficiency at the expense of preserving and protecting slow variables like memory, health, relationships, and long-term sustainability. This "erasure problem" manifests in several ways:

1. **Computational Systems**: Traditional databases are mutable; they overwrite data to save space and gain speed. The solution proposed is ekphoric synthesis—an append-only world where history is preserved, and the present is constructed as an intelligent view of that past.

2. **Economic and Societal Systems**: Our economies prioritize short-term profit growth over long-term survival and health of resources (like land, water, and human well-being). The proposed solution is "constraint first design," which means establishing hard non-negotiable constraints to protect critical slow variables before optimizing for speed or efficiency.

3. **Human Behavior**: On an individual level, we are encouraged to constantly upgrade our tools and information without considering the long-term consequences of cognitive erosion—where our ability to think deeply, connect ideas, and solve complex problems diminishes due to over-reliance on fast-variable systems (like social media feeds).

4. **Physical Systems**: In logistics and supply chains, a "just-in-time" approach is critiqued for lacking resilience; instead, there should be "slack" or redundancy—buffer inventory that acts as an immune system against disruptions.

The overarching message is that we must shift our focus from optimizing for speed to preserving the integrity of slow variables. This involves:

- **Building Decay Functions**: In economic systems, this could mean designing financial instruments (like bank accounts) with built-in decay rates to prevent hoarding and ensure circulation.
  
- **Establishing Lower Bounds on Access**: Ensuring that basic necessities are accessible regardless of wealth, preventing systemic imbalances.

- **Designing for Local Regeneration Loops**: Reducing reliance on global supply chains by producing locally where feedback loops between consumption and impact are short and unavoidable.

- **Persistence Contracts in Software Design**: Committing to backward compatibility so users can build mastery over tools without constant relearning due to frequent changes.

Ultimately, the challenge is to ask not "how fast can we go?" but "how long will this system endure?" By focusing on persistence and constraint-first design, we may be able to avoid our own erasure—both in technology and society.
