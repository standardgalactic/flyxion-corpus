This exploration delves deeply into the intersection of psychoanalysis and neuroscience through the lens of Lacanian philosophy and Carl Fristen’s predictive modeling. Here’s a synthesis of the key points:

1. **Prediction Error as Affect**: The brain processes prediction errors not just mathematically but emotionally, feeling unpleasure when it encounters unexpected states (like losing a caregiver). This emotional response is crucial for survival during early development.

2. **Brain Prioritization Function**: In moments of uncertainty—whether hungry, tired, or anxious—the midbrain acts like a decision triangle, prioritizing the problem causing the highest prediction error and adjusting brain precision to focus on solving that issue first.

3. **Repression as a Survival Mechanism**: For human infants, basic animal instincts are insufficient for navigating complex social environments. Repression allows children to create non-declarative action plans (like emotionally detaching from fear) which become deeply ingrained habits in adulthood, often leading to psychological suffering when these strategies fail.

4. **Clapper-Ed Experiment**: Edward Clapper-Ed’s experiment with a woman suffering severe amnesia demonstrated how subcortical memory can retain emotional responses without conscious recall, showing the power of non-declarative emotional memory and its impact on behavior.

5. **Juisan’s IS Surplus Prediction Error**: The concept that there is always leftover prediction error—what Juisan calls surplus prediction error—is fundamental to human existence. This residual tension drives creativity, desire, and growth rather than a state of perfect calm or homeostasis.

6. **Therapeutic Implications**: Viewing mental health through this lens suggests therapy’s goal isn’t eliminating all tension but safely provoking new prediction errors to allow the brain to adapt and rewrite more flexible models for adult life. This approach embraces uncertainty as essential to being human, rather than a flaw or weakness.

7. **Philosophical Shift**: By placing consciousness in the affective (brainstem) and the unconscious in the predictive ego of the neocortex, we challenge traditional views that reduce the mind to mere statistics. Instead, our minds are seen as inherently messy prediction machines designed for survival through unresolved tension.

In essence, this deep dive reframes mental health not as a quest for perfection or elimination of conflict but as an active engagement with one’s contradictions and uncertainties—recognizing them as vital components of being human.
