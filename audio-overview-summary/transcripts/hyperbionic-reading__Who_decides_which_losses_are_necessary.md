# hyperbionic-reading/Who_decides_which_losses_are_necessary

The passage discusses a complex socio-political analysis, focusing on how powerful entities and insulated actors maintain their influence by defunding, deregulating, and neglecting public systems they have abandoned. This phenomenon is termed "impunity," which goes beyond merely breaking the law; it represents an insensitivity to the suffering of the broader community (the "commons") while retaining political power.

The author introduces the concept of the "expensive future," a regime characterized by privatization and loss, where society splits into two distinct groups: those with private wealth ("preplace") who can replace losses from the commons with private purchases, and those without such resources ("plus plus"), whose survival is directly tied to the health and functioning of public services.

The text then delves into resistance strategies. It argues that empathy must be redefined as a structural force rather than just an emotional response. By coupling coefficients—meaning connections between individuals' fates—the suffering outside one's immediate circle becomes costly, discouraging abandonment of the commons. The author critiques modern rhetoric that frames compassion as a finite resource limited to family and nation (a concept likened to "emotional austerity"), which serves to justify leaving others vulnerable.

Historically, the bund in early 20th-century Eastern Europe offers an example with its concept of "hereness" or "doikate," emphasizing physical commitment to stay and build safety, justice, and community locally rather than seeking escape. This philosophy rejects the idea that only external solutions (like rockets to Mars) can provide security.

The essay proposes practical systemic remedies under a principle called "resolvability." It suggests shifting the burden of proof so that constraints must be demonstrated as real alternatives, not merely accepted as inevitable outcomes. Key measures include pre-registered counterfactuals—documenting all considered alternatives before crises occur—to prevent post-hoc excuses and ensure transparency.

For listeners, applying this involves challenging authority when they claim losses were necessary without evidence of no viable alternatives. This could mean demanding detailed data on alternative scenarios, preserving audit paths for future review, and empowering community monitoring with legal standing to halt harmful decisions.

The overarching theme is that uncertainty and loss are pre-allocated by insulated actors who control the narrative through monopolies over data and power structures. By building connections outside one's demographic or past data patterns—becoming "unmodelable"—individuals can reclaim some agency in shaping their future, resisting isolation and predictability imposed by current systems.

In essence, the message is a call to action for collective resistance against structural incentives that favor privatization at the expense of public welfare, emphasizing local engagement and redefining empathy as a tool for systemic change.
