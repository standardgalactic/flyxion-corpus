# computation/radiative-switch/Buildings_that_think_with_wax_and_heat

The discussion above explores the concept of xylomorphic computation—a form of computing where physical materials themselves perform computational tasks based on their constraints and interactions with the environment. Key points include:

1. **Continuous Computation**: This mode only exists while actively running, such as a mechanical governor in a steam engine adjusting throttle in real time. Once stopped (e.g., when tuning off), there is no record of past computations; it's akin to a goldfish with short-term memory.

2. **Terminal Computation**: Similar to the arch settling under gravity and locking into a static state, terminal computation finds a solution and freezes permanently without preserving its computational history or process.

3. **Accumulative (Tree Analogy)**: Trees grow continuously but lay down annual rings that preserve their past growth patterns, making them legible over time. This mode balances memory with adaptability, allowing the structure to evolve while retaining historical information.

4. **Biological Scar Tissue**: Biological scars serve as a middle ground between continuous and terminal modes by preserving significant events (like injuries) but abstracting the minutiae of healing processes into generalized memories.

5. **Computation as Containment**: The core thesis is that containment isn't about being sealed off from the environment, but maintaining structural organization despite chaotic external influences. This challenges traditional views where a container isolates its internal state from the outside world.

6. **Implications for Architecture**: If applied to buildings, this concept suggests that structures like houses equipped with radiative switch fields could be considered computers themselves, integrating computational processes into their very materiality and design rather than hosting separate intelligence within them.

7. **Urban Design Thought Experiment**: The idea extends to urban planning, proposing that an entire city built from thermally coupled materials might become a single, unified computing entity where the skyline itself exchanges heat and data across open lines of sight, potentially transforming how we perceive cities as collections of individual buildings versus one massive computational organism.

This exploration invites us to reconsider not just the technology within our spaces but also their fundamental nature—how they interact with and are influenced by their environments, and how these interactions might redefine our understanding of intelligence in architecture.
