The essay argues that these women—often referred to as “daughters of the air”—occupy a liminal space between host societies and their countries of origin, shaping hybrid identities through labor practices, community networks, and resistance movements.

Key arguments include:
- Distinction between formal and informal economies: migrant workers often operate in precarious, undocumented sectors that challenge conventional economic frameworks.
- Use of the term “air” metaphorically to denote transnational mobility and fluid identity formation.
- Examination of how these women’s labor contributes to both capitalist accumulation and social cohesion within diaspora communities.

The essay employs sociological theory (e.g., post‑colonialism, feminist standpoint) and draws examples from case studies in Southeast Asia and Latin America, illustrating mechanisms such as informal trade networks and solidarity unions that empower migrant workers.

Unresolved questions highlighted are the long‑term impact of migration policies on these women’s agency and the potential for broader economic integration versus cultural assimilation. The essay also notes limitations in data availability regarding undocumented labor markets.
