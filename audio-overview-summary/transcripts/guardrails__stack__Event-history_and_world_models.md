# guardrails/stack/Event-history and world models

The passage outlines a critical analysis of how major digital platforms—through their business models and technological choices—actively undermine the preservation of long-term digital histories. This erosion of historical data, driven by profit motives and economic scarcity rather than true informational needs, leads to what is termed “information feudalism.” Here’s a breakdown of the key points:

1. **Economic Scarcity vs. Digital Abundance**: The platforms exploit artificial scarcity in storage costs while offering seemingly abundant space (e.g., Gmail's initial gigabyte offer). This creates an illusion that preserving history is expensive, prompting users to delete or prune their digital footprints prematurely.

2. **Volume-Based Pricing Model**: Storage pricing scales with apparent volume rather than informational entropy (the actual unique data content). Highly redundant files—common in long, iterative histories—are thus priced disproportionately high, discouraging the retention of detailed historical records.

3. **Illustrative Case Study - Gmail**: The trajectory of Gmail from offering a gigabyte to imposing a rigid 15GB quota exemplifies how an initial promise of abundance was inverted into artificial scarcity. This shift forced users to discard valuable historical data due to financial constraints, even though the underlying cost of storage had plummeted.

4. **Information Feudalism**: The concept posits that by charging for every copy or instance of shared content (instead of deduplicated unique information), platforms extract rent from a perceived scarcity of memory and history. This aligns with an economic model where users are conditioned to believe their digital memories are expensive, thus justifying early collapse of historical data.

5. **Proposed Counter-Architecture - Plenum Hub**: The sources propose a cooperative system called Plenum Hub that uses content addressable storage (CAS) and delta compression to charge based on informational novelty rather than volume. This model would make incremental changes virtually free, preserving iterative reasoning without financial penalties.

6. **Benefits of Plenum Hub**:
   - **Cost Efficiency for Shared Content**: Popular or shared data improves compression for everyone, reducing marginal costs.
   - **Community Benefit**: More history preserved benefits the community by lowering storage costs and encouraging retention.
   - **Transparency**: The system would expose deduplication mechanics, educating users about the true cost of their digital memory.

7. **Implications for AI and Human Cognition**:
   - **Autoregressive AI Models**: These models struggle with long-term planning due to drift in internal states, which is exacerbated by premature history collapse.
   - **Human Users**: Being forced into early context collapse hampers complex cognition and decision-making abilities.

8. **Architectural Commutativity**: This principle suggests that solving complex problems incrementally (branching and committing) yields results as coherent as solving a monolithic problem globally, provided the event histories are preserved long enough to ensure compatibility when composed.

9. **Choice Between Architectures**:
   - **Control-Oriented Systems**: Optimized for profit through historical amnesia and lack of cognitive agency.
   - **Understanding-Oriented Systems**: Preserve history as an essential asset, promoting transparency, robustness, and coherent thought processes.

In essence, the passage argues that our reliance on current digital platforms—driven by economic incentives rather than genuine informational needs—threatens both artificial intelligence's ability to maintain long-term consistency and human cognition’s capacity for complex reasoning. The call is for a shift toward systems that value historical preservation as an asset, ensuring that users retain enough structural integrity of their thought processes to support deep, coherent understanding.
