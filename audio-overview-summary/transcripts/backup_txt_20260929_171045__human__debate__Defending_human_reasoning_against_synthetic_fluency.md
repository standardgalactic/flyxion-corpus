**Lev Vygotsky’s developmental account of genuine inner speech emphasizes that authentic human thought is highly compressed and predicative. In everyday problem-solving at work—when we’ve finally grasped a complex issue—we don’t articulate the entire solution in verbose, transitional sentences; instead, we experience an intuitive “click” where concepts lock together into a dense, structural understanding. This compressed knot represents true structural insight.

**Applying this to large language models (LLMs):**  
Traditional LLMs operate on the opposite end of the spectrum—they expand their outputs with verbose, qualified prose because they lack any underlying structural grounding or genuine comprehension. The problem isn’t that AI is “intentional” in a human sense; it’s that its articulacy itself becomes an epistemic hazard when we mistake length and fluency for depth.

**Why this matters:**  
If we treat LLMs as if they possess the same structural understanding as humans, we risk conflating their verbose outputs with genuine insight. This can lead to cognitive drift—where repeated deference to AI-generated text erodes our own pre-verbal intuition about what constitutes true understanding.

**Deontological vs. Phenomenological Approaches:**  
- **Deontological (rule-based) approach:** Treat the LLM solely as a calculator or formatter, avoiding any evaluative judgments. This prevents us from surrendering judgment to statistical probability distributions.
- **Phenomenological priority:** Maintain acute awareness of our own lived experience—knowing what it feels like to truly understand something—and use AI dynamically when needed (e.g., for critique) rather than as an authority.

**Operational Consequences:**  
Strict deontological discipline (task orientation over question orientation, using command-line pipelines instead of conversational interfaces) protects against the illusion that LLMs are intellectual peers. However, it also risks artificially restricting the utility of AI’s vast pattern synthesis capabilities.

**My Position:**  
I argue for cultivating phenomenological priority—maintaining awareness of our own embodied cognition and intuitive “click” experiences—to dynamically engage with LLMs when they serve as perturbations to our mental models (e.g., providing counter‑evaluations). This approach leverages AI’s fluency without losing the deep, structural understanding that remains uniquely human.

**Convergence Point:**  
Both perspectives acknowledge that humans possess a multi-layered cognition—deeply grounded in embodied experience—and that LLMs operate only on language. The divergence lies in how we choose to stand before this technology: building rigid “sea walls” (deontological discipline) versus learning to sail the turbulent waters while staying anchored to our own pre‑linguistic judgment.

**Final Takeaway:**  
The challenge isn’t about what AI is, but how we integrate it into our lives. Whether we erect strict boundaries or learn adaptive navigation depends on whether we value preserving genuine human epistemic sovereignty—or harnessing LLMs as powerful cognitive regulators that complement rather than replace our own structural understanding.
