The audio overview discusses how both ancient human societies and modern artificial intelligence systems manage disorder by actively suppressing individual selfishness. In prehistoric egalitarian groups, “reverse dominance” kept ambitious individuals in check through psychological pressure—mockery (the “dung practice”) and social isolation—ensuring that power did not become concentrated. When mockery failed, the community resorted to execution as a calculated political act, delegating the deed to close relatives to limit revenge cycles.

In contemporary AI, federated learning mirrors this principle: devices perform local training on their own data (stochastic gradient descent), then share only model updates with a central server that averages them without ever receiving raw data. This approach leverages differential privacy and noise injection to prevent any single update from revealing individual information while still allowing the system to converge on collective intelligence.

The discussion extends into theoretical frameworks such as the Relativistic Staler Vector Plenum (RSVP), which models order emergence through three interacting fields—capacity, attention, and entropy. These concepts suggest that both human social structures and AI algorithms operate under a universal principle of actively constraining disorder to maintain coherent systems.
