This discussion delves deeply into the concept of xylomorphic computation—a paradigm where physical materials themselves perform computational tasks based on their material properties and environmental constraints. Here’s a breakdown of the key ideas presented:

### 1. **Xylomorphic Computation and Material Constraints**

- **Constraint-Rich Systems**: The room described as an example is not just any space; it's a system with unique constraints (tiles, ceiling, melting wall). These constraints dictate how the system behaves—how it settles into specific configurations based on environmental factors like time of day and weather conditions.
  
- **Attractor Structure**: The settling into one configuration over another carries information about its environment. This is akin to how a thermostat adjusts based on temperature changes, but with much richer data encoded in physical states.

### 2. **Temporal Modes of Computation**

Flyxion outlines three temporal modes that describe how computation can be experienced by such systems:

- **Continuous Computation**: Only active while running; once stopped (e.g., turning off a laptop), all past computations vanish, leaving no record. This is like a thermostat adjusting in real-time but forgetting its history when not in use.

- **Terminal Computation**: Once the solution is found, it freezes permanently without erasing any information about how that solution was reached. The arch example illustrates this: once the perfect geometric balance is achieved, there’s no trace of the process leading to that state.

- **Accumulative Computation**: Similar to a tree's growth rings; each year adds new layers (like a scar), preserving history while allowing for continuous adaptation. This mode allows systems to remember past states and adapt over time without losing information about how they arrived at their current configuration.

### 3. **Biological Analogy and Scar Tissue**

The analogy of biological scars helps illustrate accumulative computation:

- **Scar Tissue**: Like a tree’s growth rings, scars preserve memory of specific events (e.g., injuries) but abstract the minutiae into generalized memories that are humanly readable.
  
- **Application to Architecture**: If applied to architecture, this means buildings could adapt and remember their history—how they were used over time. For instance, if a family always reads in one corner, that area might gradually alter its thermal properties to accommodate that habit.

### 4. **Computation as Containment**

The core thesis of the paper is that computation and containment are not separate entities but two sides of the same coin:

- **Cell Membrane Analogy**: Just like a biological cell membrane allows constant exchange with its environment while maintaining internal organization, xylomorphic materials allow for continuous interaction with their surroundings without losing structural integrity.
  
- **Autopoiesis**: This concept suggests that systems can self-create and maintain their identity through interactions with the world around them. A house made of phase-change materials would thus be considered a living system capable of computation.

### 5. **Implications for Urban Design**

The discussion extends to urban design, proposing:

- **City as a Thinking Entity**: If an entire city were built using xylomorphic materials, it could become one massive computational entity exchanging heat and light across its skyline.
  
- **Networked Intelligence**: This would transform the concept of individual houses into nodes within a larger network, where the city itself processes information about its environment in real-time.

### Conclusion

By redefining what we consider "computational," Flyxion challenges traditional views of architecture as merely passive containers for technology. Instead, it suggests that buildings could become active participants in their environments, capable of learning and adapting over time based on how they are used and interacted with. This radical shift opens up new possibilities for sustainable, adaptive urban spaces that respond dynamically to both local conditions and broader systemic changes.
