# backup_20260929_172331/backward-compatibility/mortal-computers

The exploration of xenobots, fungal computing, organoid intelligence, and the minimal model of mortal computing (H-PCC) through metabolism-based chemotaxis offers profound insights into how biological principles can be harnessed to advance computational systems. These examples collectively illustrate a paradigm shift toward creating autonomous, adaptive, and life-like machines that could revolutionize robotics, artificial intelligence, and even our understanding of computation itself.

1. **Xenobots**: By demonstrating self-organization, cooperative behavior, specialization, and morphogenesis without traditional evolutionary processes, xenobots highlight the potential for computational models to evolve functionalities autonomously. This suggests a future where machines can adapt their structure and functionality in response to environmental cues, paving the way for highly resilient and adaptive systems.

2. **Fungal Computing**: The use of mycelium networks as biological analogues for information processing introduces a novel approach to computing based on electrical activity and natural resilience. Fungal computing exemplifies how systems can be designed to self-repair and scale efficiently, offering pathways to develop energy-efficient computational architectures that mimic the adaptability and robustness found in nature.

3. **Organoid Intelligence**: The ability of organoids to engage in reinforcement learning tasks showcases their potential for developing adaptive neural networks capable of complex problem-solving. This aligns with the vision of neuromorphic computing systems, where artificial intelligence models can learn and evolve over time, drawing inspiration from biological processes such as self-organization and morphogenesis.

4. **Minimal Model of Mortal Computing (H-PCC)**: The homeostatic predictive coding circuit model provides a framework for simulating life-like adaptability through energy management akin to hunger and satiety in biological organisms. This approach emphasizes the integration of internal regulatory processes with external environmental interactions, suggesting that computational entities could be designed to exhibit basal cognition and adaptive behavior based on metabolic drives.

**Implications for Future Research**: These examples collectively underscore a growing trend toward biomimetic computing—designing systems that draw inspiration from biological life forms to achieve greater resilience, adaptability, and efficiency. The future of computing may involve the development of machines capable not only of executing predefined tasks but also of evolving their behaviors in response to environmental changes, much like living organisms.

**Challenges and Considerations**: While these models offer exciting possibilities, they also present significant challenges. Integrating learning and inference mechanisms into physical substrates requires careful consideration of how computational entities will interact with and adapt to their environments. The body-niche problem highlights the necessity for systems that are deeply aware of their physical embodiment and capable of adapting to specific ecological niches.

**Conclusion**: By leveraging insights from xenobots, fungal computing, organoid intelligence, and minimal models of mortal computing, researchers can push the boundaries of what is possible in artificial intelligence and robotics. These approaches promise not only more efficient and adaptive computational systems but also a deeper understanding of life itself, potentially leading to machines that are truly autonomous and capable of exhibiting intelligent behavior akin to biological organisms.
