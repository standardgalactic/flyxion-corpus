The recurring removal of Markov boundaries—those safety nets like trash bins, cooling-off periods, peer reviews, and biological sleep—is not a sign that society is deteriorating intellectually. Instead, it reveals a deeper accounting failure: organizations often mistake the reduction of waste for eliminating risk without fully recognizing the hidden costs. When these boundaries are removed, they don’t just vanish; they re-externalize their failures onto individuals who lack the resources or knowledge to compensate for the loss. This creates an inequitable burden on those unable to afford personal safety nets—whether through manual backups, legal representation, or simply having more time and technical skills.

Moreover, each boundary is not merely a passive wall but an active hypothesis about how systems fail, encoding past failures into operational protocols. Removing such boundaries strips away institutional memory of why they existed in the first place, leading to epistemological amnesia where new generations must rediscover solutions that earlier generations already solved. This isn’t just inefficient; it’s structurally inequitable.

Physically speaking, Landauer's principle reminds us that erasing information generates thermodynamic heat—cognitive waste heat in our case. When organizations “optimize” by tearing down these boundaries to save money or time, they’re essentially generating stress and hypervigilance within individuals who must now shoulder the cognitive load previously handled by systems. This manifests as burnout, increased vigilance, and a constant state of defensive workarounds.

In essence, the key takeaway is to critically examine your own environment this week: Are there instances where efficiency gains are being achieved at the cost of removing safety nets? If so, consider whether those savings might be compensating for hidden cognitive costs—like stress or burnout—that come from having to manually manage what once was automated. Rebuilding civilization’s undo stack begins with recognizing these structural failures and advocating for a more balanced approach that values long-term resilience over short-term efficiency gains.
