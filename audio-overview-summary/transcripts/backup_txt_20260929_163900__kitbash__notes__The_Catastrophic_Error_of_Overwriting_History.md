The key takeaway from these two papers is that our current systems—whether in computing, economics, or everyday life—are fundamentally designed to optimize for speed and efficiency at the expense of preserving history, stability, and long-term sustainability. This "erasure problem" manifests as a relentless drive to overwrite past data (in technology) and foundational societal values (like health, focus, relationships) with new information or instant gratification.

The proposed solutions revolve around two main concepts:

1. **Ekphoric Synthesis** – In computing, this means moving toward an append-only world where all changes are recorded rather than overwritten. This preserves history, allowing systems to maintain a view of past states and enabling recovery from errors or malicious actions without losing valuable information.

2. **Constraint First Design** – For broader societal and economic structures, this involves establishing non-negotiable constraints that protect critical slow variables (like health, access to basic necessities, and environmental sustainability) before optimizing for speed or efficiency. These constraints act as guardrails, ensuring systems remain resilient and capable of surviving disruptions.

The overarching message is a call to shift the fundamental question from "how fast can we go?" to "how long can we last?". By focusing on persistence—maintaining what already exists rather than constantly overwriting it—we can build more durable, robust systems that are less prone to catastrophic failure when faced with unforeseen challenges.

In practical terms, this means:

- **Technological**: Adopting technologies and practices (like blockchain) that inherently preserve data history.
- **Economic & Social**: Implementing policies and business models that prioritize long-term health over short-term profit gains, such as limiting wealth accumulation or ensuring access to basic needs regardless of financial status.

Ultimately, the challenge is to recognize that our current systems are engineered for erasure rather than preservation, and to consciously design alternatives that can endure over time. This shift could prevent systemic failures driven by a lack of resilience in both technological and societal domains.
