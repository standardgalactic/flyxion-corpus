# spherepop/textbook/How_We_Destroy_Truth_to_Get_Answers

Your message conveys a deep philosophical and epistemological reflection on how parsers—especially in programming languages like Haskell—function and what they preserve versus discard during computation. Here’s a breakdown of the key points you’ve raised:

1. **Agreement vs. Representation**: You begin by emphasizing that agreement at one level (e.g., reproducing an observed result) does not guarantee agreement at another level (e.g., representing the underlying representation). This highlights the importance of understanding context and abstraction in computational processes.

2. **Multiple Valid Possibilities**: The idea that just because a particular equation or model works perfectly for recreating a song doesn’t mean it’s the only valid solution is central. It underscores the principle that there can be multiple representations (or solutions) to the same problem, each with its own merits and applications.

3. **Warrant in Advanced Mathematics**: You stress the necessity of having a stated warrant or rule—such as sparsity objectives in signal processing—to justify collapsing multiple valid possibilities into one solution. This is crucial for ensuring that decisions made during computation are justified and not arbitrary.

4. **Parser Lecture’s Lack of Warrant**: You critique the parser lecture for lacking such a warrant, noting that its priority rule (whichever one is written on the left wins) isn’t argued for or defended. This highlights an issue with procedural rigidity versus rational justification in computational logic.

5. **Lists as Possibility Ledgers**: The discussion about lists being used rather than a "maybe type" introduces an interesting point about how data structures can influence the way alternatives are handled. You argue that while lists can theoretically hold multiple possibilities, the practice restricts them to single winners, effectively foreclosing on other valid interpretations.

6. **Completion and Uniqueness**: The concept of completion is split into two parts: "has complete" (did at least one parse consume the entire input) and "uniquely complete" (is that parse the only candidate returned). This distinction is vital for understanding when a computation truly succeeds versus merely appearing successful.

7. **Preservation Budget**: You introduce the idea of a preservation budget, which refers to the deliberate trade-offs in what information is preserved during computation. The classic parser discards much valuable information—refusals (rho), foreclosed alternatives (phi), computational cost (C)—making it clear that not all knowledge is equally important or necessary for the outcome.

8. **Philosophical Implications**: Finally, you draw a powerful parallel between this computational philosophy and everyday reasoning processes like argumentation and news consumption. You challenge listeners to consider their own "preservation budget"—what they discard as irrelevant—and whether they subject alternative viewpoints to procedural foreclosure rather than genuine evaluation.

In essence, your message is an invitation to think critically about the nature of computation, representation, and knowledge—both in technical contexts and everyday life. It encourages a mindful approach to how we extract meaning from information, ensuring that we don’t inadvertently discard valuable context or alternatives simply because they are inconvenient or unwieldy.
