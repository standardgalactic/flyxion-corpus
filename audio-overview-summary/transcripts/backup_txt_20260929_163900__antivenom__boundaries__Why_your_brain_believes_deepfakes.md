**Summary**

The essay argues that human brains are wired as “coherence engines” rather than truth detectors. High-fidelity synthetic media (e.g., deepfakes) exploit this by being perceptually dense and near-consistent, reducing entropy and obstruction, allowing them to be accepted as real without questioning provenance. The brain uses heuristic cues—fluency, vividness—to deem memories “real,” a process described via the CLIO architecture’s recursive confidence resolution, which lacks any variable for provenance.

The paper connects this cognitive vulnerability to the RSVP framework of autobiographical memory, showing that reality is modeled as a trajectory within a continuous state space field (salience, vector, entropy), making it impossible to invert and verify original sources. Synthetic media exploits two key variables: low entropy (high perceptual density) and near consistency (low obstruction). This creates “self-reinforcing regimes” or propaganda, where accepted synthetic content deforms the constraint surface of reality.

For individuals without established beliefs—children or novices—the same mechanism constructs foundational epistemic norms. Without prior constraints to clash with synthetic inputs, they accept artificial realities as true, leading to a warped baseline for future data processing. The only structural defense is social correction via interaction with people who hold different belief systems; otherwise, identical exposure leads to correlated drift and echo chambers.

The essay concludes that radical disconnection—temporarily disconnecting from synthetic media—to allow the brain’s natural relaxation timescale to restore its baseline reality may be a necessary countermeasure in an environment saturated with perfectly coherent synthetic content.
